Saving now, spending later

How money grows over time, how to value payments that happen in the future, and why the order of good and bad years matters.

A

Meet Alex

Alex gets a first job at 25 and saves for 40 years, until retiring at 65. At the end of each of those 40 years, Alex puts $5,000 into investments: 40 deposits in total, the first at 26 and the last at 65.

In retirement, Alex plans to take out $60,000 at the end of every year to live on, for 20 years, from age 66 to 85.

Alex's investments earn 6% a year: every $100 in the account grows by $6 each year.

The puzzle

Alex puts in $200,000 in total but plans to take out $1,200,000.

Is that possible? Where would the other $1,000,000 come from?

Alex's plan at a glance. Each small green bar is one $5,000 deposit. Each tall yellow bar is one $60,000 withdrawal. The bars are drawn to scale. Deposits happen at the end of each year, so the first is at 26 and the last at 65.

This page answers three questions

  1. How much will Alex's savings be worth at 65?

Why it matters. Laura's case has the same basic shape: money is invested first and paid out later. Learn the tools on Alex, then apply them yourself to Laura.

1

Two moves

Before answering the questions, you need two tools: moving money forward in time, and moving it back. Here they are at 6% a year.

⏩ Fast-forward

× 1.06 each year

  • $1,000 today becomes $1,060 in one year, and $1,123.60 in two.
  • Alex's first $5,000 goes in at the end of Alex's first working year, at age 26. It grows for 39 years, until 65, and becomes $48,518. The last $5,000 goes in on the day Alex retires, so it does not grow at all.

⏪ Rewind

÷ 1.06 each year

  • To have $60,000 one year from now, you need $56,604 today, because $56,604 grows to $60,000 in a year.
  • $60,000 two years away needs $53,400 today. Twenty years away: only $18,708.

3

Doing it in Excel

This section shows how to get the same answers yourself in Excel: the long way, one row per year, and the short way, with two functions.

The long way: one row per year

Works for any pattern of money in and out, even irregular amounts.

End = Start × (1 + rate) + deposit − withdrawal

The shortcuts: FV and PV

They only work when the payment and the return are the same every period.

=FV(rate, nper, pmt, [pv], [type]) =PV(rate, nper, pmt, [fv], [type])
rate
The return per period, for example 6%.
nper
The number of periods, here the number of yearly payments.
pmt
The equal payment each period. Excel's answer comes out with the opposite sign, so enter the payment with a minus sign to get a positive answer.
[pv] / [fv]
Optional. A single amount at the start (for FV) or at the end (for PV). Leave it out or use 0 if there is none.
[type]
Optional. Leave it out or use 0 for payments at the end of each period. Use 1 for payments at the start.

Examples at 6%

Excel shows cents. Everywhere else, this page rounds to the nearest dollar.

=FV(6%, 40, -5000)

gives 773,809.83: Alex's savings at 65. Here 40 is the number of deposits. Excel assumes each one comes at the end of the year, so the first grows 39 years and the last does not grow, exactly as in Section 1.

=PV(6%, 20, -60000)

gives 688,195.27: what Alex needs at 65.

=FV(6%, 10, 0, -1000)

gives 1,790.85: one amount of $1,000 left to grow for 10 years.

=PV(6%, 20, 0, -60000)

gives 18,708.28: the last withdrawal, 20 years away, rewound to 65.

Prove PV is not magic

Row 2 shows 56,603.77, row 3 shows 53,399.79 and row 21 shows 18,708.28 (Excel may show more decimals). The total in C22 is 688,195.27, the same as =PV(6%, 20, -60000). PV is simply this column, added up in one step.

Three things that trip people up

  • The minus sign. Excel gives the answer the opposite sign of the payment. Enter payments with a minus sign and the answer comes out positive.
  • Start or end? The type input decides whether payments happen at the start or the end of each period. The same payments give different answers. Check which one your problem needs.
  • Which date is "now"? With type 0, PV gives the value one period before the first payment. With type 1, it gives the value on the day of the first payment. Always know which date your number belongs to.

Build the long way and the shortcut. If they disagree, find out why before you trust either one.

Why order matters only when money comes out

Without withdrawals, Jordan and Riley end with exactly the same amount. Multiplying the same numbers in a different order gives the same answer, just as 2 × 3 = 3 × 2.

With withdrawals, Jordan's bad years come first. Every withdrawal after that comes out of a much smaller balance. Money taken out after a loss is gone for good: it is not there to grow when the good years arrive. Riley's bad years come at the very end, when only two withdrawals are left.

A return typed into a spreadsheet is a smooth version of reality. Real returns arrive one year at a time, in an order nobody knows in advance.

✓

What you learned

  1. Savings at 65. At 6%, Alex's $200,000 of deposits grows to $773,810. Most of that, $573,810, is growth.
  2. Needed at 65. Twenty withdrawals of $60,000 add up to $1,200,000, but Alex needs only $688,195 at 65. That money keeps earning while it waits to be spent: another $511,805 during retirement.
  3. What could go wrong. A steady return is a simplification. With the same total growth in a different order, a plan that works on paper can run out of money.

The puzzle's answer: yes, at 6% the plan works. The missing $1,000,000 comes from growth, before and during retirement, and Alex still has $85,615 to spare at 65.
